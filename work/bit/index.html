Summary

Bit is the app most people in Israel use to send money. A transfer takes seconds, and so does a mistake: one wrong digit in a phone number, and the money is on its way to a stranger with no way to stop it.

How it started

I found the gaps as a Bit user

In Bit, a transfer reaches the recipient as a request. No money moves until they approve it. Once they do, the money is theirs.

I learned where that breaks when someone I didn’t know sent me a large sum by mistake. I didn’t approve it. It made me think: what would have happened if I had? The sender would never learn where the money went, and getting it back would depend on a stranger’s goodwill.

It motivated me to research how people use Bit: private users, business owners, and users of competing apps. More than 50 people took part. The gap bothered people in all three groups, and some competing apps already had a check or a buffer that Bit lacked.

The two major pain points I concluded from my research brought me to design 2 new features on top of the real app, each solves a problem. One checks who a phone number belongs to before money is sent, the other keeps a short window open after sending, to change the details or retract the transfer. Each has its own section below, next to its problem.

Problem 1

Money can go to the wrong person

A recipient who isn't in the sender's contacts is added by typing a phone number. The screen tells the sender to make sure the number is right, but nothing in the app helps them do it. One wrong digit, and the money goes to someone else entirely.

The solution

Verify who the number belongs to

The app now checks the number before any money moves. When a number outside the contacts is typed, a verify button appears. Tapping it looks the number up, the way Truecaller does, or with similar technology built into Bit, and a banner says who the number belongs to, in words like “This number belongs to Moshe. Did you mean to send him money?”

The sender can then correct the number or continue. It is one small step, placed exactly where the mistake happens.

Problem 2

A transfer that can't be taken back

Once a transfer is sent, it’s out of the sender’s hands. There is no pause or cancel. The only way to stop it is for the recipient to decline, and after they approve, the money belongs to them. If it reached the wrong person, only that person’s goodwill can bring it back.

The solution

Keep a way back after sending

The idea is based on a cheque cancelation process in Israel: whoever gives one can still cancel it. The bank limits how many times a year, and each cancellation costs a fee, so it can't be abused.

A Bit transfer gets the same safety net. After the sender confirms, the transfer enters an in-process state with a ten-minute countdown, and during it the sender can confirm the transfer or change its details. The money may already have reached the recipient, and the bank handles the rest, with a fee and a limit, the way it does for a cancelled cheque. I checked that this works under banking regulation and on the back end.
